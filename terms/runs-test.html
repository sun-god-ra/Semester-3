Runs test

To pick the test, ask what the data look like. One sample or a paired sample compared with a middle value: sign test. Two independent samples: Mann–Whitney \(U\). Three or more independent samples: Kruskal–Wallis \(H\) (the \(U\) test generalised). Two numeric measurements per item and you want association: Spearman. A single sequence and you want to know if the order is random: runs test.

From Prob & Stats › Worked Examples from the Lecture Slides (Dr M. V. Rao)

The runs test checks the order of observations rather than their values. A run is a block of identical symbols, and the number of runs tells us about the pattern. Too few runs means similar values cluster together, as in a trend. Too many means the sequence alternates unnaturally, as in a cyclic pattern. A truly random arrangement is in between.

From Prob & Stats › Tests of Randomness — Runs Test (Wald–Wolfowitz)

When is the runs test used?

From Probability and Statistics Part A › question

Non-parametric (distribution-free) tests do not assume a particular distribution (such as normal) for the population, and use signs, ranks or counts rather than the actual values. They suit ordinal data, small samples and skewed data. Examples: sign test, Mann–Whitney \(U\), Kruskal–Wallis, Spearman's rank correlation, runs test. Disadvantage: less powerful than the parametric test when the parametric assumptions do hold.
