Standard deviation

  • For samples from a normal population, \(R\) has mean \(d_2\sigma\) and standard deviation \(d_3\sigma\), where \(d_2,d_3\) depend only on \(n\).

From Prob & Stats › Estimating $\sigma$ from the Range

When \(n\) is large the binomial tables are inconvenient, so we approximate with a normal distribution with mean \(n/2\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt n/2\). The 0.5 is the continuity correction, needed because a discrete count is being approximated by a continuous curve.

From Prob & Stats › The Sign Test

There is no simple formula for the area under the curve, so tables are used for the standard normal distribution only. Standardising, \(Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma\), counts how many standard deviations a value is above or below the mean, and this lets one table serve every normal distribution. The table gives the area to the left of \(z\). For negative \(z\) you can use symmetry, \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\), if your table lists only positive values.

From Prob & Stats › Normal Distribution

When \(n\) is large, calculating a binomial probability needs many terms. The shape of the binomial bars becomes bell-like as \(n\) grows, and a normal curve with the same mean \(np\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{npq}\) fits it well. The condition (\(np>15\) and \(n(1-p)>15\) in the lecture slides; looser rules of thumb like 5 or 10 appear in other books) makes sure the curve is not squashed against the wall at 0 or \(n\).

From Prob & Stats › Normal Approximation to the Binomial

This test checks a claim such as "the standard deviation of the process is at most 3 mm". The statistic compares the sample variance with the claimed variance, scaled by the degrees of freedom. A value close to \(n-1\) means the sample variance matches the claim. A much larger value suggests that the true variance is bigger, and a much smaller one suggests it is smaller.

From Prob & Stats › Hypotheses Concerning One Variance
