Degrees of freedom

  • t-distribution: bell shaped like normal but heavier tails; shape depends on \(\nu=n-1\) (degrees of freedom). \(\nu\to\infty\) ⇒ becomes normal.

From Prob & Stats › Interval Estimation

In my earlier notes I wrote that the unequal-variance small-sample test was outside the syllabus. The lecture slides show it is taught, with the estimated degrees of freedom rounded down, so it is now covered in 11f.

From Prob & Stats › Worked Examples from the Lecture Slides (Dr M. V. Rao)

When you need a lower-tail value, the reciprocal rule converts it into an upper-tail value with the degrees of freedom swapped. For a two-tailed test at level \(\alpha\), split the level into two halves and use \(F_{\alpha/2}\) with the larger variance on top.

From Prob & Stats › Hypotheses Concerning Two Variances

Only large values are evidence against \(H_0\), so the test is always right-tailed. The degrees of freedom are \(k-1\) because once \(k-1\) of the group rates are fixed, the last one is determined by the pooled rate.

From Prob & Stats › Hypotheses Concerning Several Proportions

The test statistic measures how many standard errors the sample mean is away from the value claimed by \(H_0\). A value near 0 supports \(H_0\). A value far out in the tail does not. For a large sample, or a known \(\sigma\), the statistic follows the standard normal curve. For a small sample from a normal population with unknown \(\sigma\), it follows the \(t\) curve with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

From Prob & Stats › Hypotheses Concerning One Mean
