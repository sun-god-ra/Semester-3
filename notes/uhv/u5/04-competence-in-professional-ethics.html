Competence in Professional Ethics

Universal Human Values II · Unit V: Implications on Professional Ethics

Competence in Professional Ethics
Subject
Universal Human Values II (24UC201)
Unit
Unit V: Implications on Professional Ethics
Topic
4 of 6
Terms
Professional ethics

Practice: Part B questions for Unit V and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Professional = a person who applies specialised competence (engineering, science, management) in his work for others; Professional ethics = *the right conduct in professional work, based on human values
  • Competence in professional ethics has three abilities (as in the syllabus)
    1. Ability to utilise the professional competence for augmenting the universal human order — use my engineering skill to fulfil the four goals, not only for profit
    2. Ability to identify the scope and characteristics of people-friendly and eco-friendly production systems — systems that serve people and respect nature
    3. Ability to identify and develop appropriate technologies and management patterns for the above production systems — right technologies and right management
  • Scope of people-friendly and eco-friendly production systems
    • Characteristics: meets the needs of people (physical facility of right quantity and quality) without exploiting people or nature
    • people-friendly: fair wages, safe working conditions, dignity, local employment, products that are useful and safe, no exploitation of consumers (no misleading ads)
    • eco-friendly: renewable energy and materials, recyclable (waste → input), low pollution, self-regulating (within the limits of nature), conserves resources, biodegradable
  • Appropriate technology = suited to local needs, resources, skills; small-scale, decentralised, labour-using, low-energy, repairable, durable ("Small is beautiful", E. F. Schumacher — listed in your references)
  • Management patterns = cooperative / participatory, trust-based, decentralised; profit as a means, not the only goal; mutual fulfilment of workers, customers, society, nature
Aspect Present (conventional) Holistic (proposed)
Goal profit, growth mutual fulfilment (prosperity for all)
Technology large-scale, energy-intensive, centralised appropriate, decentralised, eco-friendly
Management hierarchical, competition, exploitation cooperative, trust-based, participatory
Nature resource to exploit partner (co-existence)
Product designed for sales (obsolescence, "waste") for need; durable; recyclable
  • Case studies of typical holistic technologies, management models and production systems (illustrative; check the details from your own sources)
    • Technologies: solar cookers / solar water heaters / photovoltaic, biogas plants (cow dung → fuel + manure), rainwater harvesting, organic and natural farming (compost, no chemical), drip irrigation, bamboo/mud/low-energy housing, wind power, water purification with local materials, e-waste recycling
    • Management models: cooperatives (e.g., Amul dairy cooperative; Lijjat Papad), self-help groups, community-managed forests / panchayat, Gandhian trusteeship, Schumacher's "Buddhist economics", Kumarappa's "Economy of Permanence"
    • Production systems: village industries, decentralised production, farm-to-consumer local supply, zero-waste (closed-loop) manufacturing
    • Caution: these are examples I give from general knowledge; the course text gives its own case studies, so use those in the exam when your teacher has asked for them
Explanation

The three abilities in the syllabus are the three things an engineer should be able to do: (a) use skill to support the universal order, (b) know what people-friendly and eco-friendly production looks like, (c) develop suitable technologies and management for it. For example: instead of designing a product that is cheap now but cannot be recycled, an engineer with this competence designs it for durability, repair and recycling. A good table is present vs holistic on goal, technology, management, nature, product.

Honest note on the case studies: UHV case studies in your textbook may differ from mine. The examples I listed (biogas, solar, rainwater harvesting, Amul, cooperatives, Schumacher's appropriate technology, Kumarappa) are real and widely cited, but I have not verified how your book describes them, so treat them as supporting examples and not as definitions.
