Prescriptive Process Models

Software Engineering · Unit I: Process Models and Agile Development

Prescriptive Process Models
Subject
Software Engineering (24CS203)
Unit
Unit I: Process Models and Agile Development
Topic
6 of 13
Terms
Prototyping, Spiral model, Waterfall model, Incremental model

Practice: Part B questions for Unit I and Part A short questions.

On this page
  1. Waterfall model (classic life cycle, linear sequential)
  2. Incremental process model
  3. Evolutionary process models
  4. Specialized process models
Notebook notes
  • Prescriptive models define a set of activities, actions, tasks, milestones and work products, prescribing how the work is organised. Aim to bring order to chaos
Waterfall model (classic life cycle, linear sequential)
  • Each phase finishes completely before next; documents at each phase. V-model: variation that shows testing's relation to the earlier phases (each development phase has a matching test phase)
  • Advantages: simple, easy to manage, well-defined stages, good when requirements are fixed and clear
  • Problems: real projects rarely follow sequential flow; customer must state all requirements early; working version available late; blocking states (team waits)
Incremental process model
  • Linear sequence applied repeatedly; each increment delivers a part of the product. First increment = core product (basic requirements); later increments add features. Useful when staff is limited or early version is needed
Evolutionary process models
  • Prototyping: customer gives general objectives; build a quick-design prototype (listen to customer → quick plan → modeling quick design → construct prototype → deployment, delivery & feedback). Throw-away or evolve. Pros: clarifies requirements. Cons: customer thinks prototype is the product; poor implementation compromises are carried over
  • Spiral model (Barry Boehm): risk-driven, combines prototyping with waterfall; series of evolutionary releases; each loop (spiral) = a framework activity: communication → planning → modeling → construction → deployment. Risk analysis at every loop. Cost and schedule adjusted by feedback. Good for large, high-risk systems. Cons: needs risk-assessment expertise, costly, hard to convince customers
  • Concurrent models (concurrent development): all activities exist concurrently but are in different states (none, under development, awaiting changes, under review, under revision, baselined, done). Event-driven transitions. Used in client/server, engineering
Specialized process models
  • Component-based development: reuses pre-packaged commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) components; spiral-like; steps: research & evaluate components → consider integration → design architecture → integrate → test. Leads to reuse
  • Formal methods model: mathematical specification (e.g. Z, VDM); proof-based; very defect-free, used in safety-critical (avionics, medical); expensive, time consuming, needs experts
  • Aspect-oriented software development (AOSD): isolates crosscutting concerns (logging, security, transactions) into aspects
Explanation

The waterfall model is the oldest. It moves in a straight line from communication to deployment, completing each phase before starting the next, and produces documents along the way. It suits projects with stable and well-understood requirements. Its weaknesses are that real projects are rarely so linear, that the customer must state all the requirements at the start, and that no working version exists until late in the project, so mistakes are found when they are expensive to fix.

The incremental model applies the waterfall sequence repeatedly. The first increment delivers the core product, and later increments add features, so users get something useful early. Prototyping addresses unclear requirements: a quick prototype is built, shown to the customer, and refined until the requirements are understood. The danger is that the customer treats the prototype as the finished product, and that quick-and-dirty compromises remain in the final system.

The spiral model, proposed by Barry Boehm, is risk-driven. The project goes around a spiral, and in every loop the team determines objectives, evaluates alternatives, identifies and resolves risks (perhaps by building a prototype), develops the next level of the product, and plans the next loop. It is suited to large, high-risk systems but needs people skilled in risk assessment. Concurrent models let all the activities exist at the same time in different states, and move between states as events occur.

Specialized models serve particular cases: component-based development builds from reusable off-the-shelf components, the formal methods model uses mathematical specifications and proofs to avoid defects in safety-critical systems, and aspect-oriented development separates crosscutting concerns such as security or logging.
