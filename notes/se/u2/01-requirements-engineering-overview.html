Requirements Engineering — Overview

Software Engineering · Unit II: Requirements Engineering

Requirements Engineering — Overview
Subject
Software Engineering (24CS203)
Unit
Unit II: Requirements Engineering
Topic
1 of 13
Terms
Requirements engineering, Use case

Practice: Part B questions for Unit II and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Requirements engineering (RE) = process of understanding what the customer wants, analysing need, assessing feasibility, negotiating, specifying, validating and managing the requirements. Builds a bridge between customer needs and design/construction. Done before design
  • Requirement = a statement of what the system must do (functional) or how well (non-functional: performance, security, usability, reliability, portability)
  • Seven distinct tasks (some in parallel, adapt to the project)
Task What happens
Inception basic understanding of the problem, the people who want a solution, the nature of the solution, preliminary communication with stakeholders
Elicitation ask customers, users, others what the objectives are, what to accomplish, how it fits the business needs. Problems: scope (boundary unclear), understanding (customers not sure what they need), volatility (requirements change)
Elaboration expand and refine information from elicitation into a technical model (function, behaviour, information). Driven by creation and refinement of user scenarios (use cases)
Negotiation conflicting requirements, limited resources → rank by priority, resolve conflicts, assess cost and risk (win–win)
Specification written document, graphical models, formal math model, collection of usage scenarios, prototype — a Software Requirements Specification (SRS)
Validation review the work products for quality: errors, omissions, ambiguity, conflict, conformance to standards. Key mechanism: formal technical review
Requirements management track and control change: identification, traceability tables (requirements traceability), version control
Explanation

Requirements engineering is the work of finding out, in a disciplined way, what the system is supposed to do. It is done before design because a design built on wrong requirements is wrong, however well it is made. The seven tasks are not strictly sequential. Inception gives a first understanding, and elicitation collects what the stakeholders want, which is difficult because the scope may be unclear, the users may not know what they want, and requirements change. Elaboration turns this information into a technical model. Negotiation resolves conflicts between stakeholders and balances cost and benefit. Specification writes the result down, usually as an SRS. Validation checks it, and requirements management handles changes during the project and keeps track of where each requirement is used.
