The Critical-Section Problem

Operating Systems · Unit III: Process Synchronization and Deadlocks

The Critical-Section Problem
Subject
Operating Systems (24CS204)
Unit
Unit III: Process Synchronization and Deadlocks
Topic
2 of 15
Terms
Critical section, Mutual exclusion, Starvation, Race condition, Kernel, Real-time

Practice: Part B questions for Unit III and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Consider n processes {P0, …, P(n−1)}; each has a critical section (segment of code) where it may be changing common variables, updating a table, writing a file. When one process is in its critical section no other process is allowed to be in its critical section (mutual exclusion)
  • General structure: entry section (request permission) → critical section → exit section → remainder section
do {
    [ entry section ]
        critical section
    [ exit section ]
        remainder section
} while (true);
  • Three requirements for a solution
    1. Mutual exclusion — if process Pi is executing in its critical section, no other process can be executing in theirs
    2. Progress — if no process is in its critical section and some processes want to enter, only those not in their remainder sections can participate in deciding which enters next, and this selection cannot be postponed indefinitely
    3. Bounded waiting — there is a bound on the number of times other processes are allowed to enter their critical sections after a process has made a request to enter and before that request is granted (no starvation) - Assumption: each process executes at nonzero speed; no assumption about relative speeds of processes
  • Kernel-mode race conditions: preemptive kernels (a process can be preempted while running in kernel mode — harder to design, esp. SMP; but more responsive, suitable for real-time and less risk of a kernel process running for an arbitrarily long time) vs nonpreemptive kernels (a process runs until it exits kernel mode, blocks, or voluntarily yields → essentially free of race conditions in kernel data structures). Linux (since 2.6), Windows, Solaris, IRIX are preemptive
Explanation

When several processes read and write the same shared data, the final result may depend on who runs first. This is a race condition. The classic example is counter++ and counter--, each of which is compiled to three machine instructions (load into a register, change the register, store back). If the instructions of the two interleave, one update is lost and the counter ends as 4 or 6 instead of 5. The cure is to allow only one process at a time to touch the shared data.

The part of the code that touches shared data is the critical section. Each process has an entry section, then the critical section, then an exit section, then the remainder section. A correct solution must provide three things. Mutual exclusion means that only one process is inside at a time. Progress means that if no one is inside and someone wants to enter, only the processes that are not in their remainder sections take part in choosing who enters, and the choice cannot be delayed forever. Bounded waiting means that a limit exists on how many times others can enter before a waiting process gets its turn.

A kernel can be nonpreemptive, where a process runs in kernel mode until it leaves it, which avoids kernel race conditions but is poor for real-time response, or preemptive, which is more responsive but harder to design.
