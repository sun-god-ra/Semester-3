Background — Why Synchronisation?

Operating Systems · Unit III: Process Synchronization and Deadlocks

Background — Why Synchronisation?
Subject
Operating Systems (24CS204)
Unit
Unit III: Process Synchronization and Deadlocks
Topic
1 of 15
Terms
Race condition, Kernel, Critical section, Mutual exclusion, Real-time

Practice: Part B questions for Unit III and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Cooperating processes can affect or be affected by other processes, may share a logical address space (code and data) or share data through files/messages. Concurrent access to shared data may result in data inconsistency; maintaining data consistency requires mechanisms to ensure the orderly execution of cooperating processes
  • Producer–consumer with a counter: counter++ (producer) and counter-- (consumer) executed concurrently may give wrong values. In machine code:
    • counter++ → register1 = counter; register1 = register1 + 1; counter = register1
    • counter-- → register2 = counter; register2 = register2 - 1; counter = register2
    • Interleaving (counter = 5 initially): T0 producer register1 = counter (5); T1 producer register1 = register1 + 1 (6); T2 consumer register2 = counter (5); T3 consumer register2 = register2 − 1 (4); T4 producer counter = register1 (6); T5 consumer counter = register2 (4) → final 4 (wrong; correct = 5). Reversing the last two gives 6. Incorrect state arises because both processes manipulated the variable concurrently
  • Race condition: several processes access and manipulate the same data concurrently and the outcome depends on the particular order in which the accesses take place. To guard against it, ensure that only one process at a time manipulates the variable → process synchronisation / coordination. Race conditions are even more likely on multicore systems; also seen in OS data structures (e.g., fork() assigning next available pid in the kernel — two processes could get the same pid)
Explanation

When several processes read and write the same shared data, the final result may depend on who runs first. This is a race condition. The classic example is counter++ and counter--, each of which is compiled to three machine instructions (load into a register, change the register, store back). If the instructions of the two interleave, one update is lost and the counter ends as 4 or 6 instead of 5. The cure is to allow only one process at a time to touch the shared data.

The part of the code that touches shared data is the critical section. Each process has an entry section, then the critical section, then an exit section, then the remainder section. A correct solution must provide three things. Mutual exclusion means that only one process is inside at a time. Progress means that if no one is inside and someone wants to enter, only the processes that are not in their remainder sections take part in choosing who enters, and the choice cannot be delayed forever. Bounded waiting means that a limit exists on how many times others can enter before a waiting process gets its turn.

A kernel can be nonpreemptive, where a process runs in kernel mode until it leaves it, which avoids kernel race conditions but is poor for real-time response, or preemptive, which is more responsive but harder to design.
