The Map Method — Karnaugh Map (K-map)

Topic

Terms
K-map, Minterm, Gray code, Prime implicant

Practice: Part B questions for Unit II and Part A short questions.

On this page
  1. Two-variable map
  2. Three-variable map (rows \(x\), columns \(yz\) in order 00 01 11 10)
  3. Four-variable map (rows \(AB\), columns \(CD\))
  4. Rules of grouping (write these in the exam)
  5. Prime implicants
Notebook notes
  • A K-map = truth table redrawn as a grid so that adjacent cells differ in exactly one variable. Lets us simplify by eye (graphical method)
  • Number of cells \(=2^n\). Cell number = minterm number
  • Rows/columns labelled in Gray code (00, 01, 11, 10) — NOT binary order!
  • Adjacent cells combine because \(xy+xy'=x\) (one variable disappears)
  • Map is wrap-around: left edge touches right edge, top touches bottom, four corners touch (like a torus)
Two-variable map
\(y'\) \(y\)
\(x'\) \(m_0\) \(m_1\)
\(x\) \(m_2\) \(m_3\)
Three-variable map (rows \(x\), columns \(yz\) in order 00 01 11 10)
00 01 11 10
\(x=0\) \(m_0\) \(m_1\) \(m_3\) \(m_2\)
\(x=1\) \(m_4\) \(m_5\) \(m_7\) \(m_6\)
  • Group of 1 cell → 3 literals; 2 cells → 2 literals; 4 cells → 1 literal; 8 cells → 1 (function = 1)
  • Example: \(F=\sum m(0,2,4,5,6)\) → group \(m_0,m_2,m_4,m_6\) (all with \(z=0\)) \(=z'\); group \(m_4,m_5\) \(=xy'\) → \(F=z'+xy'\)
Four-variable map (rows \(AB\), columns \(CD\))
\(AB\backslash CD\) 00 01 11 10
00 0 1 3 2
01 4 5 7 6
11 12 13 15 14
10 8 9 11 10
  • Group of 1 → 4 literals, 2 → 3, 4 → 2, 8 → 1, 16 → function \(=1\)
Rules of grouping (write these in the exam)
  1. Group only 1s (for SOP). Group sizes are powers of 2 (1, 2, 4, 8, 16)
  2. Groups must be rectangular (including wrap-around), no diagonals
  3. Make each group as large as possible
  4. Use as few groups as possible to cover all 1s
  5. Overlap is allowed (a 1 may be in many groups)
  6. Every 1 must be covered at least once
  7. In a group, variables that change disappear; variables that stay the same remain (1 → true form, 0 → complemented)

Example (4-var): \(F(A,B,C,D)=\sum m(0,1,2,5,8,9,10)\)

  • \(F=B'D'+B'C'+A'C'D\)
Prime implicants
  • Implicant: any product term (group of 1s) that implies \(F\)
  • Prime implicant (PI): an implicant that cannot be made bigger (a maximal group). Not contained in a larger group
  • Essential PI (EPI): a PI that covers a minterm no other PI covers. Must be in the answer
  • Non-essential / selective PI: choose enough to cover what is left
  • Procedure: find all PIs → pick all EPIs → cover remaining 1s with min number of other PIs
  • A function can have more than one minimal answer
Explanation

A Boolean expression can be simplified algebraically, but this needs experience and there is no guarantee we reach the smallest form. The K-map turns the job into pattern spotting. It is the truth table written as a grid in which neighbouring cells differ by only one input, so any two neighbouring 1s can be merged into one term with one variable fewer (\(xy+xy'=x\)). The labels run 00, 01, 11, 10, a Gray sequence rather than a binary count. If you wrote 00, 01, 10, 11 instead, cells that look like neighbours would differ in two variables and the grouping would be wrong. This is the most common error in K-map answers.

The edges of the map are joined. The cells in the left column are neighbours of those in the right column, and the same holds for the top and bottom rows. That is why the four corners of a 4-variable map form a valid group of four (they are \(B'D'\) in the example). The larger the group, the more variables drop out, so we always make the biggest possible rectangles and use as few as we can.

A prime implicant is a group you cannot enlarge. An essential prime implicant contains at least one 1 that no other group covers, so it is unavoidable. After taking the essential ones, any remaining 1s are covered with as few additional groups as possible. In the example, the cells 9 and 10 each have a group of four (\(B'C'\) and \(B'D'\)), while the cell 5 can only join a pair with cell 1 or cell 7 (it has no neighbour that could form a bigger rectangle), so the pair \(A'C'D\) is the cheapest cover for it. Always check whether a different choice gives the same cost, because a function can have more than one minimal form.
