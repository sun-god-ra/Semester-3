Practice: Part B questions for Unit I and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Analog signal: continuous values (voltage varies smoothly). Digital signal: only discrete values, usually 2 → binary 0 and 1
  • Digital system = takes discrete info, processes it, stores it. Examples: computer, calculator, digital watch, phone
  • Why binary? Two voltage levels are easy to build, noise immune, easy to store and copy without loss
  • Bit = binary digit. Nibble = 4 bits. Byte = 8 bits. Word = machine-dependent (16/32/64 bits)
  • Block diagram of a digital computer (Mano)
  • A computer is built from combinational circuits (output depends only on present input) and sequential circuits (output depends on present input + stored past state)
  • Logic levels: HIGH (1) ≈ +5 V / 3.3 V, LOW (0) ≈ 0 V. Positive logic: High = 1. Negative logic: High = 0
Explanation

A digital system represents everything with a finite set of symbols, almost always the two symbols 0 and 1. The advantage is robustness. A voltage near 5 V and a voltage near 0 V are easy to tell apart, so small amounts of electrical noise do not change the stored value, and copies are exact. Analog signals carry information in the exact voltage, so noise directly corrupts them.

A digital computer is made from two kinds of circuits. Combinational circuits have no memory, so their output depends only on what is applied right now, as in an adder. Sequential circuits contain storage elements, so the output depends on the inputs and on what happened earlier, as in a counter. The whole of this subject is about these two kinds, which is why units 2 and 3 cover combinational design and units 4 and 5 cover sequential design.
