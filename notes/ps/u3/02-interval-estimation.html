Interval Estimation

Probability and Statistics · Unit III: Statistical Inference

Interval Estimation
Subject
Probability and Statistics (24MA203)
Unit
Unit III: Statistical Inference
Topic
2 of 11
Terms
Confidence interval, T-distribution, Degrees of freedom

Practice: Part B questions for Unit III and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Interval estimate = range of values + confidence level. Called confidence interval (CI).
  • \((1-\alpha)100\%\) CI means: if we repeat sampling many times, \((1-\alpha)100\%\) of such intervals contain true \(\mu\).
  • Not "prob. that \(\mu\) is in this interval is 95%" (μ is fixed, interval is random) ← common mistake

Large sample (\(n\ge30\), σ known, or use \(s\)):

\[\bar x\ \pm\ z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\]

Small sample (\(n<30\), σ unknown, population normal):

\[\bar x\ \pm\ t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}\]

Confidence \(\alpha\) \(z_{\alpha/2}\)
90% 0.10 1.645
95% 0.05 1.96
98% 0.02 2.33
99% 0.01 2.575
  • Higher confidence → wider interval. Bigger \(n\) → narrower interval.
  • t-distribution: bell shaped like normal but heavier tails; shape depends on \(\nu=n-1\) (degrees of freedom). \(\nu\to\infty\) ⇒ becomes normal.
df \(t_{0.05}\) \(t_{0.025}\) \(t_{0.01}\) \(t_{0.005}\)
5 2.015 2.571 3.365 4.032
8 1.860 2.306 2.896 3.355
10 1.812 2.228 2.764 3.169
15 1.753 2.131 2.602 2.947
20 1.725 2.086 2.528 2.845
30 1.697 2.042 2.457 2.750

Example: \(n=50,\ \bar x=78.3,\ \sigma=6.2\), 95% CI

  • \(E=1.96\times\frac{6.2}{\sqrt{50}}=1.72\)
  • CI = \((76.58,\ 80.02)\)
Explanation

A confidence interval reports the estimate together with its uncertainty. The 95% does not describe this particular interval. It describes the method: if you repeated the whole sampling process many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would capture the true mean. Once you have computed one interval, the true mean is either inside it or not, so a statement like "there is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is in \((76.58, 80.02)\)" is technically wrong.

Three things change the width. A higher confidence level needs a bigger multiplier \(z_{\alpha/2}\), so the interval gets wider. A larger sample reduces \(\sigma/\sqrt n\), so the interval gets narrower. A larger population spread \(\sigma\) makes it wider again.

When the sample is small and \(\sigma\) is not known, you have to estimate \(\sigma\) with \(s\), and this adds extra uncertainty. The \(t\) distribution accounts for it by having heavier tails than the normal curve, so the multiplier is bigger. As the degrees of freedom grow, the \(t\) values approach the \(z\) values, which is why \(z\) is good enough for large samples.
