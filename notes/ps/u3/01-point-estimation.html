Point Estimation

Probability and Statistics · Unit III: Statistical Inference

Point Estimation
Subject
Probability and Statistics (24MA203)
Unit
Unit III: Statistical Inference
Topic
1 of 11
Terms
Variance, Central limit theorem, Unbiased estimator

Practice: Part B questions for Unit III and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Statistic = number computed from a sample. Parameter = number describing the whole population.
  • Estimator = formula/rule. Estimate = value you get from the data.
  • Point estimate = single number guess. \(\bar x\) estimates \(\mu\), \(s^2\) estimates \(\sigma^2\), \(\hat p\) estimates \(p\).
  • Good estimator: unbiased (\(E(\hat\theta)=\theta\)), small variance, consistent
    • \(E(\bar X)=\mu\) ✓ unbiased
    • \(s^2=\dfrac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1}\) is unbiased for \(\sigma^2\) (that's why divide by \(n-1\))
  • Standard error of mean: \(\sigma_{\bar X}=\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\)
  • Maximum error of estimate (prob. \(1-\alpha\)):

\[E=z_{\alpha/2}\cdot\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\]

  • Sample size needed for error at most \(E\):

\[n=\left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{E}\right)^{2}\quad(\text{always round UP})\]

  • Central Limit Theorem (CLT): for large \(n\) (\(\ge30\)), \(\bar X\) is approx. normal with mean \(\mu\), sd \(\sigma/\sqrt n\), whatever the population shape.
Explanation

Statistics is about learning about a whole population from a small sample. A point estimate is the simplest way to do that: you compute one number from the sample and use it as your best guess for the unknown parameter. The sample mean is the usual guess for the population mean.

An estimator is judged by how it behaves over many possible samples. "Unbiased" means that its average over all samples equals the true value. That is the reason the sample variance divides by \(n-1\) instead of \(n\): dividing by \(n\) would give estimates that are on average a little too small. Even an unbiased estimator is never exactly right for one sample, and the standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) tells you the typical size of the miss.

The maximum error formula turns this into a planning tool. If you want your estimate to be within \(E\) of the truth with 95% confidence, solve the formula for \(n\). Always round up, because rounding down would give a smaller sample than required.
