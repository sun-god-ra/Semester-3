Notebook notes
11a. Sampling distribution of the mean

Paint cans. \(\mu=513.3,\ \sigma=31.5,\ n=40\). \(P(510<\bar X<520)\):

  • \(\sigma_{\bar X}=\frac{31.5}{\sqrt{40}}=4.98\); \(z_1=-0.66,\ z_2=1.35\)
  • \(P=\Phi(1.35)-\Phi(-0.66)=0.9115-0.2546=\mathbf{0.6569}\)
11b. Confidence intervals for one mean
  • \(n=100,\ \sigma=5.1,\ \bar x=21.6\), 95%: \(21.6\pm1.96\times0.51\Rightarrow\mathbf{(20.6,\ 22.6)}\)
  • Nanopillars: \(n=50,\ \bar x=305.58,\ s^2=1366.86\), 99% (large sample, so \(z\)): \(305.58\pm2.575\sqrt{\frac{1366.86}{50}}=305.58\pm13.46\Rightarrow\mathbf{(292.12,\ 319.04)}\)
    • Slide slip: it writes 305.8 in the second line; the mean is 305.58.
  • Grinding balls: \(n=16,\ \bar x=3.42,\ s=0.68\), 99% (small sample, \(t\)): \(t_{0.005}(15)=2.947\), so \(3.42\pm2.947\times0.17=\mathbf{(2.92,\ 3.92)}\) g
    • Slide slip: it writes "2.942" in one place; the table value is 2.947. The interval is unchanged to two decimals.
11c. Tests on one mean (five-step method)

1. Cement brick. \(H_0:\mu=0.340\), \(H_1:\mu\ne0.340\), \(n=35\), \(\bar x=0.343\), \(\sigma=0.010\), \(\alpha=0.05\).

  • Reject if \(|Z|>1.96\). \(Z=\frac{0.343-0.340}{0.010/\sqrt{35}}=\mathbf{1.77}\)
  • \(1.77<1.96\): fail to reject \(H_0\). We cannot say the conductivity differs.

2. Vinyl flooring (p-value). \(H_0:\mu=4.5\), \(\sigma=1.5\), \(n=25\), \(\bar x=3.9\). \(Z=\frac{3.9-4.5}{1.5/5}=-2.00\).

  • Two-sided p-value \(=2\times0.0228=\mathbf{0.0455}\). Small, so the second shift's mean is probably not 4.5.
  • Slide slip: it writes "0.0228 + 0.228 = 0.0456". Should be \(0.0228+0.0228=0.0456\).

3. Truck tires. \(H_0:\mu\ge28{,}000\), \(H_1:\mu<28{,}000\), \(n=40\), \(s=1348\), \(\alpha=0.01\). Reject if \(Z<-2.33\).

  • Slide: uses \(\bar x=27{,}463\), giving \(Z=\frac{27463-28000}{1348/\sqrt{40}}=-2.52\), p \(=0.0059\), reject.
  • Problem text says \(\bar x=24{,}463\). With that value \(Z=\frac{24463-28000}{213.1}=-16.6\), still reject.

⚠ The statement and the working disagree (24,463 vs 27,463). The classic textbook version has 27,463. In the exam, use the number given in the question. The decision is "reject" either way.

4. Ribbon (small sample). \(H_0:\mu=180\), \(H_1:\mu<180\), \(n=5\), \(\bar x=169.5\), \(s=5.7\), \(\alpha=0.01\). \(t_{0.01}(4)=3.747\). \(t=\frac{169.5-180}{5.7/\sqrt5}=\mathbf{-4.12}<-3.747\): reject; p \(=0.0073\). Breaking strength is below specification.

11d. Two independent large samples

Peak electricity use, with and without a/c. \(n_1=45,\ \bar x=204.4,\ s_1^2=13825.3\); \(n_2=55,\ \bar y=130.0,\ s_2^2=8632.0\).

  • 95% CI for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\): \(74.4\pm1.96\sqrt{\frac{13825.3}{45}+\frac{8632}{55}}=74.4\pm1.96\sqrt{464.2}=74.4\pm42.2=\mathbf{(32.2,\ 116.6)}\)

Wire resistance. \(H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0.050\), \(H_1:\mu_1-\mu_2>0.050\); \(n_1=n_2=32\), \(\bar x=0.136,\ s_1=0.004\); \(\bar y=0.083,\ s_2=0.005\); \(\alpha=0.05\).

  • \(Z=\frac{0.136-0.083-0.050}{\sqrt{0.004^2/32+0.005^2/32}}=\frac{0.003}{0.001132}=\mathbf{2.65}>1.645\): reject, the claim is supported.
  • Slide wording slip: second sample is described as "standard wire" twice; the second is the alloyed wire.

Light bulbs. \(H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0\), \(H_1:\mu_1-\mu_2>0\); \(n_1=n_2=40\); \(\bar x=1647,\ s_1=27\); \(\bar y=1638,\ s_2=31\). \(Z=\frac{9}{\sqrt{27^2/40+31^2/40}}=\mathbf{1.38}<1.645\): fail to reject. The data do not substantiate the claim.

11e. Two independent small samples, equal variances (pooled t)

Coal from two mines. \(H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2\), \(H_1:\mu_1\ne\mu_2\), \(\alpha=0.01\), df \(=5+6-2=9\), \(t_{0.005}(9)=3.250\).

  • \(\bar x=8230,\ \bar y=7940\); \(s_1^2=15750,\ s_2^2=10920\)
  • \(s_p^2=\frac{4(15750)+5(10920)}9=13066.7\), \(s_p=114.31\)
  • \(t=\frac{8230-7940}{114.31\sqrt{\frac15+\frac16}}=\frac{290}{69.2}=\mathbf{4.19}>3.250\): reject. The mines differ.

Two alloys. \(n_1=58,\ \bar x=70.70,\ s_1=1.80\); \(n_2=27,\ \bar y=76.13,\ s_2=2.42\). 95% CI for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).

  • Pooled: \(s_p^2=\frac{57(1.8)^2+26(2.42)^2}{83}=4.06\), \(s_p=2.015\); \(t_{0.025}(83)\approx1.99\)
  • \(-5.43\pm1.99\times2.015\sqrt{\frac1{58}+\frac1{27}}=-5.43\pm0.94=\mathbf{(-6.37,\ -4.49)}\)
  • Large-sample (z) version: \(-5.43\pm1.96\sqrt{\frac{3.24}{58}+\frac{5.856}{27}}=-5.43\pm1.02=(-6.45,\ -4.41)\). Nearly the same.
  • Slide slip: it writes \((1.42)^2\) instead of \((2.42)^2\) in the pooled formula, but the final interval printed \((-6.4,-4.5)\) is correct.
  • Interpretation: we are 95% confident that alloy 2 has mean strength 4.5 to 6.4 units higher than alloy 1.
11f. Unequal variances, small samples (Welch's \(t'\))

This is in the syllabus: do not skip it.

\[t'=\frac{\bar x-\bar y-\delta_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}},\qquad \nu=\frac{\left(\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\dfrac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1}+\dfrac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}}\]

  • Round \(\nu\) down to an integer. Then use \(t_{\alpha/2}(\nu)\).
  • Use when the sample variances look clearly different (the F test of Unit IV decides).

Green gasoline, two catalysts. \(n_1=n_2=9\). Catalyst 1: \(\bar x=1.334,\ s_1^2=0.4548\). Catalyst 2: \(\bar y=3.766,\ s_2^2=0.1089\). \(\alpha=0.05\), \(H_1:\mu_1\ne\mu_2\).

  • \(\nu=\frac{(0.0505+0.0121)^2}{0.0505^2/8+0.0121^2/8}=11.62\to\mathbf{11}\); \(t_{0.025}(11)=2.201\)
  • \(t'=\frac{1.334-3.766}{\sqrt{0.0626}}=\frac{-2.432}{0.2502}=\mathbf{-9.71}<-2.201\): reject.
  • 95% CI: \(-2.432\pm2.201\times0.2502=-2.432\pm0.551=\mathbf{(-2.98,\ -1.88)}\)

Slide slip: the first line prints \(\bar x=0.334\); it is \(1.334\) (the sum of the readings is 12.01, over 9).

11g. Matched pairs (paired t)

Idea. Same units measured twice (before/after). Work with differences \(D_i=x_i-y_i\) and treat them as a single sample.

  • \(H_0:\mu_D=\mu_{D,0}\) (usually 0); \(t=\dfrac{\bar D-\mu_{D,0}}{s_D/\sqrt n}\) with \(n-1\) d.f. (small \(n\)); use \(z\) if \(n\) is large.
  • CI: \(\bar D\pm t_{\alpha/2}\dfrac{s_D}{\sqrt n}\)

Safety program, worker-hours lost (10 plants). Before: 45 73 46 124 33 57 83 34 26 17; After: 36 60 44 119 35 51 77 29 24 11.

  • Differences: 9, 13, 2, 5, −2, 6, 6, 5, 2, 6; sum 52, \(\bar D=\mathbf{5.2}\)
  • \(\sum(D_i-\bar D)^2=149.6\), \(s_D^2=16.62\), \(s_D=\mathbf{4.08}\)
  • \(H_0:\mu_D=0\), \(H_1:\mu_D>0\), \(\alpha=0.05\): reject if \(t>t_{0.05}(9)=1.833\)
  • \(t=\frac{5.2}{4.08/\sqrt{10}}=\mathbf{4.03}>1.833\): reject; the program is effective.
  • 95% CI: \(5.2\pm t_{0.025}(9)\frac{4.08}{\sqrt{10}}=5.2\pm2.262\times1.29=5.2\pm2.92=\mathbf{(2.28,\ 8.12)}\)

⚠ Slide slip: the slide builds the 95% CI with \(1.833\) (that is \(t_{0.05}\), a one-sided value) and gets \((2.8,\ 7.7)\). A two-sided 95% interval needs \(t_{0.025}=2.262\), giving \((2.28,\ 8.12)\). (1.833 would give a 90% interval.)

11h. Hypothesis-testing language
  • \(H_0\) (null) is the negation of what we want to establish. \(H_1\) or \(H_a\) (alternative) is what we want to establish.
  • Type I error: rejecting \(H_0\) when it is true. Probability \(=\alpha\) (level of significance).
  • Type II error: failing to reject \(H_0\) when \(H_1\) is true. Probability \(=\beta\).
Accept \(H_0\) Reject \(H_0\)
\(H_0\) true correct Type I (\(\alpha\))
\(H_0\) false Type II (\(\beta\)) correct
  • Critical regions for \(\mu_0\): \(H_1:\mu<\mu_0\Rightarrow Z<-z_\alpha\); \(\mu>\mu_0\Rightarrow Z>z_\alpha\); \(\mu\ne\mu_0\Rightarrow Z<-z_{\alpha/2}\) or \(Z>z_{\alpha/2}\). Same pattern with \(t\) for small samples.
  • "Fail to reject" is not "prove \(H_0\)". It means the data are not strong enough to reject it.
Explanation

The patterns here repeat in exams. For any test: write \(H_0\) and \(H_1\), state \(\alpha\), write the rejection rule before computing, compute the statistic, then state the decision in words about the real situation.

How to choose the method: one mean with known \(\sigma\) or large \(n\) gives \(z\); small \(n\) with unknown \(\sigma\) gives \(t\); two independent samples give the pooled \(t\) when the variances look alike and Welch's \(t'\) when they don't; the same units measured twice give the paired \(t\) on the differences. Using the two-sample test on paired data is a classic mistake, because it ignores the pairing and throws away power.

The slide slips listed above are genuine errors in arithmetic or in the problem statement. The most important ones are the 90%-vs-95% mistake in the paired confidence interval and the tire example, where the statement and the working disagree. Copying either into the exam would lose marks.

In my earlier notes I wrote that the unequal-variance small-sample test was outside the syllabus. The lecture slides show it is taught, with the estimated degrees of freedom rounded down, so it is now covered in 11f.
