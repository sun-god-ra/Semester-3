Unit
Unit III: Statistical Inference
Topic

Terms
Variance, Central limit theorem

Practice: Part B questions for Unit III and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Population vs sample: population = the whole set we study; sample = a subset picked to represent it.
  • Parameter = a number describing the population (\(\mu,\sigma\)). Statistic = a number computed from the sample (\(\bar x,s\)).
Population (parameter) Sample (statistic)
Mean \(\mu=\dfrac{\sum X}{N}\) \(\bar x=\dfrac{\sum x}{n}\)
Variance \(\sigma^2=\dfrac{\sum(X_i-\mu)^2}{N}\) \(s^2=\dfrac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1}\)
SD \(\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\) \(s=\sqrt{s^2}\)
  • Finite population (can be counted, like students of a university) vs infinite population.
  • Random sample, finite population of size \(N\): every subset of \(n\) elements has the same chance of being picked.
  • Random sample, infinite population with density \(f(x)\): each \(X_i\) has density \(f\) and the \(X_i\) are independent.

Theorem (mean and variance of \(\bar X\)). Population mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2\).

  • \(E(\bar X)=\mu\) always.
  • Infinite population (or sampling with replacement): \(\text{Var}(\bar X)=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\)
  • Finite population of size \(N\), sampling without replacement: \(\text{Var}(\bar X)=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\cdot\dfrac{N-n}{N-1}\). The factor \(\frac{N-n}{N-1}\) is the finite population correction. It is close to 1 when \(n\) is small compared with \(N\).

Law of Large Numbers. For independent \(X_i\) with the same mean \(\mu\): \(P(|\bar X-\mu|>\epsilon)\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\) for any fixed \(\epsilon>0\). Larger samples make \(\bar X\) more and more likely to be close to \(\mu\).

Central Limit Theorem. \(Z=\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\) has a distribution that approaches the standard normal as \(n\to\infty\), whatever the shape of the population.

LLN says \(\bar X\) lands near \(\mu\). CLT says how it is spread around \(\mu\) (normal, sd \(\sigma/\sqrt n\)). Don't mix them in answers.

Unbiased and more efficient estimators.

  • \(\hat\theta\) is unbiased if \(E(\hat\theta)=\theta\) for every value of \(\theta\).
  • \(\hat\theta_1\) is more efficient than \(\hat\theta_2\) if both are unbiased and \(\text{Var}(\hat\theta_1)\le\text{Var}(\hat\theta_2)\), with strict inequality for at least one \(\theta\).
  • Example: for a normal population, both \(\bar X\) and the sample median are unbiased for \(\mu\), but \(\bar X\) has the smaller variance, so \(\bar X\) is more efficient.

Point estimate of a mean. Estimator \(\bar X\); estimated standard error \(\dfrac{s}{\sqrt n}\).

Mean and SD of the numbers 0 to 100 (population). \(N=101\), \(\mu=50\), \(\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{101^2-1}{12}}=\mathbf{29.155}\).

Why \(n-1\) (slide demo). Sample \(\{1,3,29,34,40,42,48,52,68,85\}\), \(n=10\), \(\bar x=40.2\).

Divisor / centre \(\sum\) squares Result
around \(\bar x=40.2\), divide by 10 6067.6 \(\sqrt{606.76}=24.63\)
around true \(\mu=50\), divide by 10 7028 \(\sqrt{702.8}=26.51\)
around \(\bar x\), divide by \(n-1=9\) 6067.6 \(\sqrt{674.18}=25.96\)
  • The sum of squares is smallest around \(\bar x\) (6067.6 < 7028). Measured from the true centre it is larger, so dividing by \(n\) makes \(s\) too small on average. Dividing by \(n-1\) corrects that shortfall on average.

⚠ Slide slip: the slide shows "\(\sqrt{70.28}=26.51\)" but \(7028/10=702.8\), and \(\sqrt{702.8}=26.51\).

Explanation

This section fills in the language that Unit III quietly relies on. A parameter belongs to the population and is fixed but unknown; a statistic comes from your sample and changes from sample to sample. Everything in the later topics is about how much a statistic can wander (its standard error) and what that lets us say about the parameter.

The finite population correction matters only when you sample a large share of the population. If you draw 40 out of 1000, the factor is \(\frac{960}{999}\approx0.96\), so ignoring it changes little. If you draw 500 out of 1000 it is about 0.5, and ignoring it overstates the spread a lot.

The \(0..100\) demo shows the \(n-1\) idea with numbers: the squares around \(\bar x\) always add up to less than the squares around any other point (including the true \(\mu\)), so a divisor of \(n\) would on average underestimate the spread.
