Binomial Distribution

Probability and Statistics · Unit I: Probability Distributions and Random Variables

Binomial Distribution
Subject
Probability and Statistics (24MA203)
Unit
Unit I: Probability Distributions and Random Variables
Topic
2 of 6
Terms
Bernoulli trial, Variance, Binomial distribution

Practice: Part B questions for Unit I and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Bernoulli trial = only 2 results: success (prob \(p\)) / failure (prob \(q=1-p\))
  • Binomial = \(n\) Bernoulli trials, \(X\) = no. of successes
  • Conditions (write in exam!):
    • fixed no. of trials \(n\)
    • each trial → only success/failure
    • trials are independent
    • \(p\) same in every trial

\[b(x;n,p)={}^{n}C_x\,p^{x}q^{\,n-x},\quad x=0,1,\dots,n\]

  • Mean \(\mu=np\)
  • Variance \(\sigma^2=npq\)
  • Mode ≈ \(\lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor\)
  • \(p=0.5\) → symmetric. \(p<0.5\) → skewed right.
  • Sum of all probabilities: \((q+p)^n=1\)

Example: 12 items, each defective with \(p=0.1\)

  • \(P(X=2)={}^{12}C_2(0.1)^2(0.9)^{10}=\mathbf{0.2301}\)
  • \(P(X\ge 2)=1-P(0)-P(1)=\mathbf{0.3410}\)
  • \(P(X=0)=(0.9)^{12}=\mathbf{0.2824}\)
  • trick: "at least" → use complement

Bar chart: Binomial \(n=5,\ p=0.5\) (symmetric shape).

Explanation

The binomial distribution answers the question: "if I repeat the same yes/no experiment \(n\) times independently, how many times will I get a yes?" The four conditions (fixed \(n\), two outcomes, independence, constant \(p\)) matter, because exam questions often include a case where one of them fails, and then binomial is not the right model.

The formula has three parts. \({}^nC_x\) counts the ways to choose which \(x\) trials are successes, \(p^x\) is the chance that those trials all succeed, and \(q^{n-x}\) is the chance that the rest all fail. The mean \(np\) is intuitive (10 tosses of a fair coin give about 5 heads). The variance \(npq\) is largest when \(p=0.5\), because that is when the outcome is hardest to predict.

When a question says "at least" or "more than", writing the complement is usually faster. For \(P(X\ge2)\) you compute \(1-P(0)-P(1)\) instead of adding up many terms up to \(n\).
