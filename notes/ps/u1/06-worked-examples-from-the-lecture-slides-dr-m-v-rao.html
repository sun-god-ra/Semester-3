Notebook notes

These are the examples the lecturer solved in class. Exam questions are often lifted from them, so practise every one.

6a. Discrete random variable: find \(k\), mean, variance

Q. \(X\) = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7 with \(P(X)=k,\,3k,\,5k,\,7k,\,9k,\,11k\). Find \(k\), \(E(X)\), \(P(X\ge3)\), Var, SD.

  • \(36k=1\Rightarrow k=\frac1{36}\)
  • \(E(X)=\frac{1+6+15+28+54+77}{36}=\frac{181}{36}=\mathbf{5.028}\)
  • \(P(X\ge3)=1-\frac{1+3}{36}=\frac{32}{36}=\mathbf{0.8889}\)
  • \(E(X^2)=\frac{1+12+45+112+324+539}{36}=\frac{1033}{36}=28.694\)
  • \(\text{Var}(X)=28.694-(5.028)^2=\mathbf{3.416}\), so \(\sigma=\mathbf{1.848}\)

⚠ Slide slip: the slide rounds early (\(28.69-25.30=3.39\), \(\sigma=1.84\)). Keep the fractions and you get the exact \(3.416\) and \(1.848\). Marks go for the method, but write the exact value.

Q. \(X\) = number of heads in 4 tosses of a fair coin. \(P(X)=\frac1{16},\frac4{16},\frac6{16},\frac4{16},\frac1{16}\).

  • \(P(X<2)=\frac5{16}=0.3125\)
  • \(P(1<X\le3)=\frac{6+4}{16}=0.625\)
  • \(E(X)=2\), \(E(X^2)=\frac{80}{16}=5\), Var \(=5-4=1\), \(\sigma=1\)
  • Check with the shortcut: \(npq=4\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac12=1\) ✓

Q. 3 books are chosen from 6, of which 2 are defective. \(X\) = number of defective books chosen. This is hypergeometric with \(N=6,\ a=2,\ n=3\).

\(x\) 0 1 2
\(P(X=x)\) \(\frac{\binom20\binom43}{\binom63}=\frac4{20}=0.2\) \(\frac{\binom21\binom42}{20}=\frac{12}{20}=0.6\) \(\frac{\binom22\binom41}{20}=\frac4{20}=0.2\)
  • \(E(X)=0.6+0.4=\mathbf1\), \(E(X^2)=0.6+0.8=1.4\), Var \(=1.4-1=\mathbf{0.4}\), \(\sigma=0.632\)
  • Check: mean of hypergeometric is \(n\frac aN=3\cdot\frac26=1\) ✓

Q. \(x=0..7\), \(P=0,\,k,\,2k,\,2k,\,3k,\,k^2,\,2k^2,\,7k^2+k\). Find \(k\), mean, variance, and the smallest \(x\) with \(P(X\le x)>0.5\).

  • Sum \(=1\): \(10k^2+9k=1\Rightarrow 10k^2+9k-1=0\Rightarrow(10k-1)(k+1)=0\Rightarrow k=\mathbf{0.1}\) (reject \(k=-1\), probability cannot be negative)
  • So \(P=0,\,0.1,\,0.2,\,0.2,\,0.3,\,0.01,\,0.02,\,0.17\)
  • \(E(X)=\mathbf{3.66}\), Var \(=\mathbf{3.404}\), \(\sigma=\mathbf{1.845}\)
  • Cumulative: 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, ... At \(x=3\) the cdf is exactly 0.5 and "\(>0.5\)" needs strictly more, so the answer is \(x=\mathbf4\) (cdf 0.8).

Q. Mean of a given table: \(x=0,1,2,3\) with \(P=0.18,0.50,0.29,0.3\) gives \(\mu=1.17\).

⚠ Slide slip: these probabilities add to \(1.27\), not 1, so it is not a valid distribution. The lecturer's \(1.17\) is just the arithmetic of the numbers as printed. In the exam, always check \(\sum P=1\) first.

Q. Redirects per mail delivery: \(x=0..4\), \(f=0.05,0.20,0.45,0.20,0.10\).

  • \(\mu=0+0.2+0.9+0.6+0.4=\mathbf{2.1}\)
  • \(\sigma^2=\sum(x-\mu)^2f=0.2205+0.242+0.0045+0.162+0.361=\mathbf{0.99}\approx1\), \(\sigma\approx1\)
6b. Continuous random variable examples

Q1. \(f(x)=k(1-x^2)\) for \(0\le x\le1\).

  • \(\int_0^1k(1-x^2)dx=k\left(1-\frac13\right)=1\Rightarrow k=\frac32\)
  • \(P(0.1<X<0.2)=\frac32\Big[x-\frac{x^3}3\Big]_{0.1}^{0.2}=\frac32(0.19733-0.09967)=\mathbf{0.1465}\)
  • \(P(X>0.5)=\frac32\Big[x-\frac{x^3}3\Big]_{0.5}^{1}=\frac32(0.6667-0.4583)=\mathbf{0.3125}\)
  • \(E(X)=\frac32\int_0^1(x-x^3)dx=\frac32\cdot\frac14=\mathbf{0.375}\)
  • \(E(X^2)=\frac32\int_0^1(x^2-x^4)dx=\frac32\cdot\frac2{15}=0.2\)
  • Var \(=0.2-0.375^2=\mathbf{0.0594}\), \(\sigma=\mathbf{0.2437}\)

⚠ Slide slip: the slide prints \(P(0.1<X<0.2)=0.0166\), but the correct value is \(0.1465\) (the second answer, 0.3125, is right). Verify: the pdf is about 1.5 near 0, and the interval is 0.1 wide, so the answer must be near \(0.15\).

Q2. \(f(x)=ke^{-x}\), \(x\ge0\). \(\int_0^\infty ke^{-x}dx=k=1\), so \(k=1\) and mean \(=1\), Var \(=1\), \(\sigma=1\) (this is exponential with \(\lambda=1\)).

Q3. \(f(x)=ke^{-|x|}\), \(-\infty<x<\infty\).

  • \(2k\int_0^\infty e^{-x}dx=2k=1\Rightarrow k=\frac12\)
  • mean \(=0\) (odd function), Var \(=E(X^2)=2\) (gives \(2\int_0^\infty\frac12x^2e^{-x}dx=\Gamma(3)=2\))
  • \(P(0<X<4)=\frac12(1-e^{-4})=\mathbf{0.4908}\)

Q4. Distribution function \(F(x)=0\) for \(x\le1\), \(k(x-1)^4\) for \(1\le x\le3\), \(1\) for \(x>3\).

  • \(F(3)=1\Rightarrow k\cdot2^4=1\Rightarrow k=\frac1{16}\)
  • pdf \(=F'(x)=\frac{(x-1)^3}{4}\) on \([1,3]\)
  • Mean \(=\int_1^3x\frac{(x-1)^3}4dx=\mathbf{2.6}\), \(E(X^2)=6.8667\), Var \(=\mathbf{0.1067}\), \(\sigma=0.3266\)

Q5. \(f(x)=\frac{\sin x}2\), \(0\le x\le\pi\). Find mean, mode, median and \(P(0<X<\frac\pi2)\).

  • Symmetric about \(\frac\pi2\) so mean = median = mode = \(\frac\pi2\)
  • Median \(M\): \(\int_0^M\frac{\sin x}2dx=\frac12\Rightarrow1-\cos M=1\Rightarrow M=\frac\pi2\)
  • \(P(0<X<\frac\pi2)=\frac12(1-\cos\frac\pi2)=\mathbf{0.5}\)

⚠ Slide slip: the slide writes "Mean = Mode = ½ = Median". The value is \(\frac\pi2\approx1.571\), not \(\frac12\). The slide's "\(\frac12\)" is the probability (half the area to the left), not the median value.

6c. Binomial worked examples
  • Relay towers (\(p=0.9\) repaired in 1 hr, 3 breakdowns): \(P(X=0,1,2,3)=0.001,\ 0.027,\ 0.243,\ 0.729\). Expand \((S+F)^3\) with \(S=0.9,F=0.1\).
  • Solar installations (\(p=0.6\), \(n=5\)): (a) \(b(4;5,0.6)=\binom54(0.6)^4(0.4)=\mathbf{0.259}\); (b) at least 4: \(0.259+0.078=\mathbf{0.337}=1-B(3;5,0.6)=1-0.6630\)
  • Columns (\(p=0.05\), \(n=16\)): (a) at most two fail: \(B(2;16,0.05)=\mathbf{0.9571}\); (b) at least four: \(1-B(3;16,0.05)=1-0.9930=\mathbf{0.0070}\)
  • LCD panels (\(p=0.3\), \(n=18\)): \(P(X=6)=B(6)-B(5)=0.7217-0.5344=\mathbf{0.1873}\), or directly \(\binom{18}6(0.3)^6(0.7)^{12}\)
  • Hard drives (\(p=0.1\), \(n=20\), 5 failed): \(P(X\ge5)=1-B(4;20,0.1)=1-0.9568=\mathbf{0.0432}\). A probability this small makes the claim "only 10% fail" doubtful, so the data refute the claim.

Method for "at least": \(P(X\ge k)=1-P(X\le k-1)\). The cdf table gives \(B(k-1)\), never \(B(k)\). Most lost marks here come from off-by-one.

Mean and variance of a binomial (from the slides)

  • \(\mu=np\), \(\sigma^2=npq\).
  • Histogram comparison: \(n=4,p=0.5\) gives \(\mu=2,\sigma=1\); \(n=16,p=0.5\) gives \(\mu=8,\sigma=2\). The bigger \(n\) spreads wider, since \(\sigma\propto\sqrt n\).
6d. Hypergeometric worked examples
  • Batteries (\(N=24,a=6,n=3\)): \(h(0)=0.4032\); \(h(1)=0.4536\); at least two \(=1-0.8568=\mathbf{0.1432}\)
  • Conventions (\(N=16\) cities, \(a=7\) western, \(n=3\)): none western \(=\frac{\binom93}{\binom{16}3}=\frac{84}{560}=\mathbf{0.15}\); all western \(=\frac{\binom73}{560}=\frac{35}{560}=\mathbf{0.0625}\)
  • Union members, binomial as approximation (\(n=8\), \(p=\frac aN=0.8\), \(x=5\)):
\(N\) \(a\) hypergeometric \(h(5;8,a,N)\) binomial \(b(5;8,0.8)\)
30 24 0.1452 0.1468
150 120 0.1472 0.1468
300 240 0.1470 0.1468
  • As \(N\) grows, the hypergeometric value gets close to the binomial. Rule of thumb: the binomial is a good substitute when the sample is less than about 5% of the population.
  • Car chargers (\(N=20,a=5,n=10,x=2\)): hypergeometric \(=\mathbf{0.3483}\); binomial with \(p=0.25\) gives \(0.2816\). They differ a lot because the sample (10) is half of the population (20), so the draws are strongly dependent. Justification: use the hypergeometric when sampling without replacement from a small lot.
6e. Poisson worked examples
  • Bindery (\(p=0.05\), \(n=100\), \(x=2\)): binomial \(=0.081\); Poisson with \(\lambda=5\) gives \(\frac{e^{-5}5^2}{2!}=\mathbf{0.084}\) (table: \(F(2;5)-F(1;5)=0.125-0.040=0.085\))
  • Generators (\(n=3840\), \(p=\frac1{1200}\), \(\lambda=3.2\)): \(f(0..10)=0.041,\ 0.130,\ 0.209,\ 0.223,\ 0.178,\ 0.114,\ 0.060,\ 0.028,\ 0.011,\ 0.004,\ 0.002\). The peak is at \(x=3\) (just below \(\lambda\)).
  • Bad checks (\(\alpha=6\)/day): (a) 4 in one day: \(\lambda=6\), \(f(4;6)=\mathbf{0.134}\); (b) 10 in two days: \(\lambda=12\), \(f(10;12)=\mathbf{0.105}\)
  • Tin plate (\(\alpha=0.2\)/min):
    • one imperfection in 3 min: \(\lambda=0.6\), \(f(1;0.6)=0.878-0.549=\mathbf{0.329}\)
    • at least 2 in 5 min: \(\lambda=1\), \(1-F(1;1)=1-0.736=\mathbf{0.264}\)
    • at most 1 in 15 min: \(\lambda=3\), \(F(1;3)=e^{-3}(1+3)=\mathbf{0.199}\)
  • Poisson-process link (from the slides): split interval \(T\) into tiny pieces of length \(\Delta t\). In each piece \(P(\text{success})=\alpha\Delta t\), more than one is negligible, and pieces are independent. That makes the count binomial with \(n=T/\Delta t\), \(p=\alpha\Delta t\), so \(\lambda=np=\alpha T\).
6f. Extra properties used in problems
  • Expectation of a sum / constant: \(E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b\), \(\text{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\text{Var}(X)\). Adding a constant does not change the variance.
  • Mean of a general distribution: \(\mu=\sum x f(x)\); variance \(\sigma^2=\sum(x-\mu)^2f(x)=E(X^2)-\mu^2\) (the two forms are the same; the second is quicker).
  • Uniform on \(1..n\): \(f(x)=\frac1n\), \(\mu=\frac{n+1}2\), \(\sigma^2=\frac{n^2-1}{12}\).
  • Mean-Mode-Median: for a moderately skewed curve, Mean \(-\) Mode \(\approx3(\text{Mean}-\text{Median})\) (Pearson's empirical relation). On the slides it was used only as a check on the symmetric case.
Explanation

This section does not add new theory. It collects the lecturer's examples so you can see the patterns: (1) use \(\sum P=1\) or \(\int f=1\) to find the unknown constant; (2) use the constant to find mean and variance through \(E(X)\) and \(E(X^2)\); (3) for "at least" and "at most" questions, convert to a cdf difference or a complement.

Three things are worth keeping in mind. First, always sanity-check an answer by shape. A pdf that starts near 1.5 over a 0.1-wide interval cannot give a probability of 0.0166, which is how the slide slip is caught. Second, "smallest \(x\) with cdf \(>0.5\)" really is strict, so a cdf of exactly 0.5 does not qualify. Third, when the sample is a large share of the population (chargers example), the binomial is a poor stand-in for the hypergeometric. The union-members table shows the gap closing as \(N\) grows.

The slide slips are listed so you do not copy them into the exam. Where a slip changes the answer (0.0166 vs 0.1465; \(\frac12\) vs \(\frac\pi2\)), the corrected value was recomputed here by integration.
