The Sign Test

Probability and Statistics · Unit V: Non-Parametric Statistical Tests

The Sign Test
Subject
Probability and Statistics (24MA203)
Unit
Unit V: Non-Parametric Statistical Tests
Topic
2 of 7
Terms
P-value, Continuity correction, Sign test, Normal distribution, Standard deviation

Practice: Part B questions for Unit V and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Uses only the sign (+ or −) of each difference. Ignores size.
  • One-sample: \(H_0\): median \(=\tilde\mu_0\). Replace each value \(>\tilde\mu_0\) by +, \(<\) by −. Drop ties (values equal to \(\tilde\mu_0\)), reduce \(n\).
  • Paired (two-sample): take \(d_i=x_i-y_i\), record sign. \(H_0\): \(P(+)=P(-)=\tfrac12\)
  • Under \(H_0\), number of plus signs \(X\sim\) Binomial\((n,\tfrac12)\)
  • Decision
    • \(H_1\): median \(>\tilde\mu_0\) → P-value \(=P(X\ge x_+)\)
    • \(H_1\): median \(<\tilde\mu_0\) → P-value \(=P(X\le x_+)\)
    • \(H_1\): \(\ne\) → double the smaller tail
    • reject if P-value \(\le\alpha\)
  • Large \(n\) (>25 or so): normal approximation with continuity correction

\[z=\frac{(x\pm0.5)-n/2}{\sqrt n/2}\]

  • use \(x+0.5\) if \(x<n/2\), \(x-0.5\) if \(x>n/2\)

Example 1 (one-sample): \(H_0\): median \(=20\); data 22, 18, 25, 27, 19, 31, 24, 26, 21, 30, 17, 28

  • plus = 9, minus = 3, \(n=12\), no ties
  • \(P(X\le3)=0.0730\), two-sided \(=0.146>0.05\) → fail to reject

Example 2 (paired): 14 pairs → 11 plus, 2 minus, 1 tie. \(n=13\)

  • \(P(X\le2)=0.0112\), two-sided \(=0.0225<0.05\) → reject \(H_0\) (two treatments differ)

Example 3 (normal approx): \(n=40\), \(x=12\) plus, \(H_1\): \(p<\tfrac12\)

  • \(z=\dfrac{12.5-20}{\sqrt{40}/2}=\dfrac{-7.5}{3.162}=-2.37\), \(P=0.0089\) → reject at 5%
Explanation

The sign test is the simplest test there is. For one sample, we ask whether the sample median equals a claimed value. If it does, each observation is equally likely to fall above or below it, so the count of observations above is binomial with \(p=\tfrac12\). For paired data we do the same on the differences. Values equal to the claimed median give no sign and are removed, which lowers \(n\). Forgetting to remove ties is a common error.

In Example 1 there are 9 plus signs and 3 minus signs from 12 values. Nine out of twelve looks lopsided, but the binomial probability of seeing 3 or fewer minus signs is 0.073, and for a two-sided test we double it to 0.146. That is not small enough, so the sample does not give strong evidence against the claimed median. In Example 2 only 2 minus signs out of 13 gives 0.0225 after doubling, which is below 0.05, so the treatments differ.

When \(n\) is large the binomial tables are inconvenient, so we approximate with a normal distribution with mean \(n/2\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt n/2\). The 0.5 is the continuity correction, needed because a discrete count is being approximated by a continuous curve.
