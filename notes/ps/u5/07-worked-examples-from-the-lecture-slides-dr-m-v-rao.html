Notebook notes
7a. Sign test

Rule from the slides. To test \(\tilde\mu=\tilde\mu_0\): write \(+\) for values above \(\tilde\mu_0\), \(-\) for values below, and discard values equal to \(\tilde\mu_0\). Under \(H_0\) the signs are binomial trials with \(p=0.5\). Reject if the tail probability is \(\le\alpha\).

Octane ratings (\(n=15\)). \(H_0:\tilde\mu=98.0\), \(H_1:\tilde\mu>98.0\), \(\alpha=0.01\).

  • One value equals 98.0 and is dropped, so \(n=14\).
  • Signs: 12 pluses and 2 minuses. \(P(X\ge12)=1-P(X\le11)=1-0.9935=\mathbf{0.0065}\)
  • \(0.0065<0.01\): reject \(H_0\); the median octane rating exceeds 98.0.
  • (The slide writes "\(1-P(x<11)\)"; it means \(1-P(x\le11)\).)

Safety programme (10 plants, before \(-\) after): signs \(+++ + - + + + + +\), so 9 pluses. \(P(X\ge9)=1-0.9893=\mathbf{0.0107}<0.05\): reject; the programme is effective.

The same data gave \(t=4.03\) in the paired \(t\) test and \(r_s=0.988\) in the Spearman test. All three methods reject. The sign test uses only the direction of each difference, so it is the weakest of the three but needs no normality assumption.

7b. Mann–Whitney \(U\) test

Steps (from the slides):

  1. Rank all \(n_1+n_2\) values together (ties get the average rank).
  2. \(W_1,W_2\) = rank sums of the two samples.
  3. \(U_1=W_1-\dfrac{n_1(n_1+1)}2\) and \(U_2=W_2-\dfrac{n_2(n_2+1)}2\) (check \(U_1+U_2=n_1n_2\)).
  4. \(\mu_{U_1}=\dfrac{n_1n_2}2\), \(\sigma^2_{U_1}=\dfrac{n_1n_2(n_1+n_2+1)}{12}\), \(Z=\dfrac{U_1-\mu_{U_1}}{\sigma_{U_1}}\)
  5. \(H_1\): population 1 stochastically larger ⇒ reject if \(Z>z_\alpha\); population 2 larger ⇒ reject if \(Z<-z_\alpha\); "not identical" ⇒ reject if \(|Z|>z_{\alpha/2}\).

Two sands (\(n_1=15,\ n_2=14\), \(\alpha=0.01\), two-sided).

  • Combined ranks give \(W_1=162\) (Sand I ranks: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 19, 20, 29) and \(W_2=273\). Check: \(162+273=435=\frac{29\cdot30}2\) ✓
  • \(U_1=162-\frac{15\cdot16}2=\mathbf{42}\), \(\mu_{U_1}=105\), \(\sigma^2=\frac{15\cdot14\cdot30}{12}=525\), \(\sigma=22.91\)
  • \(Z=\frac{42-105}{22.91}=\mathbf{-2.75}<-2.575\): reject \(H_0\). The two sands are not identical.
  • Slide slip: in the sorted list "1.48" is a typo for 0.48.
7c. Kruskal–Wallis \(H\) test

\[H=\frac{12}{n(n+1)}\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{R_i^2}{n_i}-3(n+1)\]

  • Valid (chi-square approximation) when every \(n_i>5\); d.f. \(=k-1\). Reject if \(H>\chi^2_\alpha(k-1)\).

Corrosion methods (pit depths): A: 77 54 67 74 71 66; B: 60 41 59 65 62 64 52; C: 49 52 69 47 56. \(n=18\).

  • Rank sums: \(R_A=84,\ R_B=55.5,\ R_C=31.5\) (tied 52s share rank 4.5)
  • \(H=\frac{12}{18\cdot19}\left(\frac{84^2}6+\frac{55.5^2}7+\frac{31.5^2}5\right)-3(19)=\mathbf{6.67}\) (slide rounds to 6.7)
  • \(\chi^2_{0.05}(2)=5.991\). \(6.67>5.991\): reject; the three methods are not equally effective.
7d. Spearman rank correlation

Same safety-programme data (before / after).

Before 45 73 46 124 33 57 83 34 26 17
Rank \(R_i\) 5 8 6 10 3 7 9 4 2 1
After 36 60 44 119 35 51 77 29 24 11
Rank \(S_i\) 5 8 6 10 4 7 9 3 2 1
  • Only two ranks differ (3 vs 4, 4 vs 3), so \(\sum d^2=2\) and \(r_s=1-\frac{6\cdot2}{10\cdot99}=\mathbf{0.988}\)
  • Slide method (product form): \(\sum R_iS_i=384\); \(r_s=\dfrac{\sum R_iS_i-\frac{n(n+1)^2}4}{\frac{n(n^2-1)}{12}}=\dfrac{384-302.5}{82.5}=0.988\) ✓ (same)
  • Test: \(H_0\): independent, \(H_1\): not independent. The slide uses \(Z=\sqrt n\,r_s=\sqrt{10}(0.988)=3.12>1.65\): reject.

⚠ Two points. (1) Many textbooks use \(z=r_s\sqrt{n-1}=0.988\times3=2.96\). Both exceed the critical value, so the decision is the same. In an answer for this lecturer, use \(\sqrt n\) as taught and mention \(\sqrt{n-1}\) as the common alternative. (2) The slide says "reject if \(Z>z_\alpha=1.65\)" for a hypothesis that is two-sided in words. A strict two-sided test uses \(\pm1.96\), which still rejects.

  • Properties: \(-1\le r_s\le1\); near \(+1\) large \(X\) pairs with large \(Y\); near \(-1\) the opposite; \(r_s\) measures a monotone (not necessarily linear) relationship.
7e. Runs test (randomness)
  • Normal approximation (from the slides) when neither \(n_1\) nor \(n_2\) is below 10: \(\mu_u=\dfrac{2n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}+1\), \(\sigma_u=\sqrt{\dfrac{2n_1n_2(2n_1n_2-n_1-n_2)}{(n_1+n_2)^2(n_1+n_2-1)}}\), \(Z=\dfrac{u-\mu_u}{\sigma_u}\). For smaller counts use the table of critical run counts.

Defective (d) and non-defective (n) pieces: nnnnnddddnnnnnnnnnnddnndddd, \(\alpha=0.01\), \(H_1\): not random.

  • Count: 27 symbols; \(n_1=17\) n's, \(n_2=10\) d's; runs: nnnnn, dddd, nnnnnnnnnn, dd, nn, dddd, so \(u=6\)
  • \(\mu_u=\frac{2\cdot10\cdot17}{27}+1=13.59\), \(\sigma_u=2.37\)
  • \(Z=\frac{6-13.59}{2.37}=\mathbf{-3.20}<-2.575\): reject; the arrangement is not random (too few runs, defectives are clustered).

Lathe shaft diameters (40 values): is the setting changed too often (a frequently alternating pattern)?

  • Median \(=0.250\). Write \(a\) for above, \(b\) for below, and drop the 5 values equal to the median, leaving 35 symbols: aababababaababaabbaabaabbababbababa
  • \(n_1=19\) (a), \(n_2=16\) (b), \(u=27\)
  • \(\mu_u=\frac{2\cdot19\cdot16}{35}+1=18.37\), \(\sigma_u=2.89\), \(Z=\frac{27-18.37}{2.89}=\mathbf{2.98}\)
  • Alternating pattern means too many runs, so the rejection region is in the upper tail: reject if \(Z>z_{0.01}=2.33\). \(2.98>2.33\) ⇒ reject randomness.

⚠ Slide slip: the criterion line prints "\(Z<z_\alpha=2.33\)". For "too many runs" it must be \(Z>2.33\). The conclusion on the slide (reject) is right.

Direction guide: few runs ⇒ clustering or trend ⇒ lower tail (\(Z\) very negative). Many runs ⇒ alternating or cyclic ⇒ upper tail (\(Z\) very positive). A plain "not random" test uses both tails.

Explanation

All five tests in this unit follow the same idea: they do not use the measured values directly, but only signs, ranks, or run counts. That is why they need no normal-population assumption. The price is that they use less information, so they are a little less powerful than the corresponding \(t\) test when the normal assumption actually holds.

To pick the test, ask what the data look like. One sample or a paired sample compared with a middle value: sign test. Two independent samples: Mann–Whitney \(U\). Three or more independent samples: Kruskal–Wallis \(H\) (the \(U\) test generalised). Two numeric measurements per item and you want association: Spearman. A single sequence and you want to know if the order is random: runs test.

The safety-programme data appear three times in the course (paired \(t\), sign test, Spearman). They all reject, which is a useful check that your calculation is sensible. Watch the two real traps. In the sign test, drop the values equal to the median before counting \(n\). In the runs test, decide which tail you need before computing: clustering means few runs, alternation means many.

The lecturer's Spearman test uses \(\sqrt n\) where many books use \(\sqrt{n-1}\). I could not settle from the slides alone which one the exam expects, so write the slide version, and add a line that \(\sqrt{n-1}\) gives the same decision here.
