Notebook notes
9a. Confidence interval for a variance

Glass refractive index. \(n=20\), \(s^2=1.20\times10^{-4}\). 95% CI for \(\sigma\).

  • d.f. \(=19\); \(\chi^2_{0.025}=32.852\) and \(\chi^2_{0.975}=8.907\) (the slide prints 8.901; the table value is 8.907)
  • \(\dfrac{19(1.2\times10^{-4})}{32.852}<\sigma^2<\dfrac{19(1.2\times10^{-4})}{8.907}\)
  • \(6.94\times10^{-5}<\sigma^2<2.56\times10^{-4}\), so \(\mathbf{0.0083<\sigma<0.0160}\)

Remember the flip: the larger \(\chi^2\) goes under the lower limit.

9b. Test on one variance

Silicon wafers. \(H_0:\sigma=0.50\), \(H_1:\sigma>0.50\), \(n=15\), \(s=0.64\), \(\alpha=0.05\).

  • Reject if \(\chi^2>\chi^2_{0.05}(14)=23.685\)
  • \(\chi^2=\frac{14(0.64)^2}{(0.50)^2}=\frac{5.734}{0.25}=\mathbf{22.94}<23.685\): cannot reject.
  • Conclusion: even though the sample SD (0.64) is above 0.50, the evidence is not enough to call the process unsatisfactory.

⚠ Slide slips: one line prints \(22.34\) instead of \(22.94\), and the final sentence says the sample SD "exceeds 0.64"; it should be "exceeds 0.50". The decision does not change.

9c. Test on two variances (F test)

Rule. For \(H_1:\sigma_1^2\ne\sigma_2^2\), put the larger sample variance on top and use \(F_{\alpha/2}(\nu_{\text{top}},\nu_{\text{bottom}})\). For a one-sided test, put on top the variance that \(H_1\) says is larger.

⚠ The slide describes the numerator as "the variance of the larger sample". It means the larger variance, not the larger sample size. In the coal example below, Mine 2 has more readings but Mine 1 has the larger variance, and the slide correctly puts Mine 1 on top.

Silver plating. \(n_1=n_2=12\), \(s_1=0.035\), \(s_2=0.062\). \(H_0:\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2\), \(H_1:\sigma_1^2<\sigma_2^2\), \(\alpha=0.05\).

  • Reject if \(F=\frac{s_2^2}{s_1^2}>F_{0.05}(11,11)=2.82\)
  • \(F=\frac{(0.062)^2}{(0.035)^2}=\mathbf{3.14}>2.82\): reject. Company 1's plating is less variable.

Coal mines. \(s_1^2=15750\) (\(n_1=5\)), \(s_2^2=10920\) (\(n_2=6\)). \(H_1:\sigma_1^2\ne\sigma_2^2\), \(\alpha=0.02\).

  • \(F=\frac{15750}{10920}=\mathbf{1.44}\) with \((4,5)\) d.f.; \(F_{0.01}(4,5)=11.39\)
  • \(1.44<11.39\): cannot reject. The equal-variance assumption in the pooled \(t\) test of Unit III is reasonable.

Confidence interval for \(\sigma_2^2/\sigma_1^2\)

\[F_{1-\alpha/2}(n_1-1,n_2-1)\frac{s_2^2}{s_1^2}<\frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2}<F_{\alpha/2}(n_1-1,n_2-1)\frac{s_2^2}{s_1^2}\]

  • Use \(F_{1-\alpha/2}(\nu_1,\nu_2)=\dfrac1{F_{\alpha/2}(\nu_2,\nu_1)}\) to get the lower F value.
  • Green gasoline, 98% CI (\(n_1=n_2=9\), \(s_1^2=0.4548\), \(s_2^2=0.1089\); ratio \(\frac{s_2^2}{s_1^2}=0.2394\)): \(F_{0.01}(8,8)=6.03\) and \(F_{0.99}(8,8)=\frac1{6.03}=0.166\), so the interval is \((0.166\times0.2394,\ 6.03\times0.2394)=\mathbf{(0.040,\ 1.44)}\). It contains 1, so equal variances cannot be ruled out.

Slide slip: the slide prints \(s_2^2=0.1.89\); the correct value is \(0.1089\).

9d. Estimating a proportion
  • Sample proportion \(\hat p=\dfrac xn\); \(E(\hat p)=p\), \(\text{SD}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}\).
  • Large-sample CI: \(\hat p\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n}\); the margin is the maximum error \(E\).

Examples

  • \(x=36,\ n=100\), 95%: \(0.36\pm1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.36\times0.64}{100}}=0.36\pm0.094=\mathbf{(0.266,\ 0.454)}\) (slide shows 0.26 from truncation)
  • \(x=136,\ n=400\), 99%, maximum error: \(E=2.575\sqrt{\frac{0.34\times0.66}{400}}=\mathbf{0.061}\). Slide slip: it prints \((0.64)\) for \(0.66\).

Sample size

  • \(p\) known (roughly): \(n=p(1-p)\left(\dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}}E\right)^2\)
  • \(p\) unknown (worst case \(p=\frac12\)): \(n=\dfrac14\left(\dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}}E\right)^2\)
  • Adobe bricks, 95%, \(E=0.04\): (a) no idea of \(p\): \(\frac14\left(\frac{1.96}{0.04}\right)^2=600.25\to\mathbf{601}\); (b) \(p\le0.12\): \(0.12\times0.88\times\left(\frac{1.96}{0.04}\right)^2=253.5\to\mathbf{254}\). Round up.
9e. Tests on proportions

One proportion (Bluetooth transceivers). \(H_0:p=0.70\), \(H_1:p>0.70\), \(n=60\), \(x=48\), \(\alpha=0.05\).

  • \(Z=\frac{x-np_0}{\sqrt{np_0(1-p_0)}}=\frac{48-42}{\sqrt{12.6}}=\mathbf{1.69}>1.645\): reject. (Slide wording slip: "0.95 level of significance" should be "0.05".)

Two proportions (tractors). \(\hat p_1=\frac{16}{200}=0.08\), \(\hat p_2=\frac{14}{400}=0.035\), \(\alpha=0.01\), \(H_1:p_1>p_2\).

  • Pooled \(\hat p=\frac{16+14}{600}=0.05\)
  • \(Z=\dfrac{0.08-0.035}{\sqrt{0.05\times0.95\left(\frac1{200}+\frac1{400}\right)}}=\dfrac{0.045}{0.01887}=\mathbf{2.38}>2.33\): reject. The second line does better.
  • (The slide prints "\(-\)" between \(\frac1{200}\) and \(\frac1{400}\); it must be "+".)
  • 95% CI for \(p_1-p_2\): \(0.045\pm1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.08\times0.92}{200}+\frac{0.035\times0.965}{400}}=0.045\pm0.0417=\mathbf{(0.003,\ 0.087)}\)

Note on consistency: the test pools the proportions (since \(H_0\) says they are equal), the CI does not pool.

9f. Several proportions (chi-square)
  • \(k\) samples, \(x_i\) successes of \(n_i\). Pooled \(\hat p=\frac{\sum x_i}{\sum n_i}\).
  • Expected counts: \(e_{1j}=n_j\hat p\) (successes) and \(e_{2j}=n_j(1-\hat p)\) (failures). Shortcut: (row total × column total) / grand total.
  • \(\chi^2=\sum\dfrac{(O-e)^2}{e}\), d.f. \(=k-1\). Reject if \(\chi^2>\chi^2_\alpha\).

Three materials, crumbling. Observed crumbled 41, 27, 22 of 120, 80, 100. Expected crumbled \(36,24,30\); intact \(84,56,70\).

  • \(\chi^2=\frac{25}{36}+\frac9{24}+\frac{64}{30}+\frac{25}{84}+\frac9{56}+\frac{64}{70}=\mathbf{4.575}\)
  • Critical \(\chi^2_{0.05}(2)=5.991\). \(4.575<5.991\): cannot reject. The three materials crumble at about the same rate.

Four disk methods. Superconducting: 31, 42, 22, 25 out of 50 each (120 total). Expected 30 and 20 in every column.

  • \(\chi^2=\frac1{30}+\frac{144}{30}+\frac{64}{30}+\frac{25}{30}+\frac1{20}+\frac{144}{20}+\frac{64}{20}+\frac{25}{20}=\mathbf{19.5}\)
  • \(\chi^2_{0.05}(3)=7.815\). \(19.5>7.815\): reject; the methods differ.
  • Individual 95% CIs (the slide asks to plot them): method 1: (0.485, 0.755); method 2: (0.738, 0.942); method 3: (0.302, 0.578); method 4: (0.361, 0.639). Method 2 stands out as best, method 3 as worst.
9g. \(r\times c\) tables
  • Homogeneity (column totals fixed): \(H_0:p_{i1}=p_{i2}=\dots=p_{ic}\) for every row \(i\).
  • Independence (only the grand total fixed): \(H_0:p_{ij}=p_{i\cdot}\,p_{\cdot j}\).
  • Both use the same calculation: \(e_{ij}=\dfrac{(\text{row }i\text{ total})(\text{col }j\text{ total})}{n}\), \(\chi^2=\sum\dfrac{(O-e)^2}e\), d.f. \(=(r-1)(c-1)\).

Motor repair shops (homogeneity, 100 motors per shop). Observed:

Shop 1 Shop 2 Shop 3 Total
Complete 78 56 54 188
Adjustment 15 30 31 76
Incomplete 7 14 15 36
  • Expected per shop: \(62.67,\ 25.33,\ 12.00\). \(\chi^2=\mathbf{15.17}\), d.f. \(=4\), \(\chi^2_{0.05}(4)=9.488\) ⇒ reject; repair quality differs between shops.
  • Slide slips: the text says Shop 3 had "55" complete, but the table (and the total of 100) needs 54; the slide also prints the first-row expected as 62.7 (rounded from 62.67).

Training programme vs job success (independence, \(n=400\), \(\alpha=0.01\)).

Below avg Average Above avg Total
Poor 23 60 29 112
Average 28 79 60 167
Very good 9 49 63 121
Total 60 188 152 400
  • Expected: row 1: 16.80, 52.64, 42.56; row 2: 25.05, 78.49, 63.46; row 3: 18.15, 56.87, 45.98
  • Contributions \(\frac{(O-e)^2}{e}\): row 1: 2.29, 1.03, 4.32; row 2: 0.35, 0.00, 0.19; row 3: 4.61, 1.09, 6.30
  • \(\chi^2=\mathbf{20.18}\), d.f. \(=4\), \(\chi^2_{0.01}(4)=13.277\) ⇒ reject independence.
  • The biggest contributions (6.30 for "very good + above average", 4.61 for "very good + below average", 4.32 for "poor + above average") show where the dependence is: good trainees do better on the job, and weak trainees do worse.
Explanation

Three checks save most marks in this unit. (1) For a variance interval, the larger \(\chi^2\) value goes in the denominator of the lower limit. (2) For the F test, the bigger variance goes on top, and the d.f. follow the numerator first. (3) For any chi-square table test, make sure the expected counts add up to the same row and column totals as the observed table.

When you say "reject", state what it means in the real situation: "the second production line does better", "the methods differ", "performance and success are dependent". The marking scheme usually gives a mark for this sentence.

The slide slips were all arithmetic or typing errors. The one that matters conceptually is the F-test rule. The phrase "larger sample" on the slide is wrong in general, since the F ratio is set by which sample variance is larger. For a two-sided test you always make the ratio at least 1 by putting the bigger variance on top, which is why the critical value is read at \(\alpha/2\).
