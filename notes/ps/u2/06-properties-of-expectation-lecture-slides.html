Properties of Expectation (Lecture Slides)

Probability and Statistics (24MA203)
Unit
Unit II: Probability Densities and Joint Distributions
Topic
6 of 7
Terms
Variance, Confidence interval

Practice: Part B questions for Unit II and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • For a function \(g(X)\): discrete \(E[g(X)]=\sum g(x_i)f(x_i)\), continuous \(E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f(x)\,dx\).
  • \(E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b\) and \(\text{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\text{Var}(X)\). The shift \(b\) drops out of the variance.
  • \(E(a_1X_1+\dots+a_kX_k)=a_1E(X_1)+\dots+a_kE(X_k)\) (always true).
  • \(\text{Var}(a_1X_1+\dots+a_kX_k)=a_1^2\text{Var}(X_1)+\dots+a_k^2\text{Var}(X_k)\) (only if the \(X_i\) are independent).
  • Coefficient of variation \(\delta=\dfrac{\sigma}{\mu}\) — a unit-free measure of spread (lecture slides use it to compare variability across different units).

Proof 1: standardised variable has mean 0, variance 1. \(Z=\frac{X-\mu}\sigma\).

  • \(E(Z)=\frac1\sigma E(X)-\frac\mu\sigma=\frac\mu\sigma-\frac\mu\sigma=0\)
  • \(\text{Var}(Z)=\frac1{\sigma^2}\text{Var}(X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}=1\)

Proof 2: sum and difference of independent variables.

  • \(E(X_1\pm X_2)=\mu_1\pm\mu_2\)
  • \(\text{Var}(X_1\pm X_2)=\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2\) — variances add even for a difference (because \((-1)^2=1\)).

Example. \(X_1\): mean 4, var 9; \(X_2\): mean \(-2\), var 5; independent.

  • \(E(2X_1+X_2-5)=8-2-5=\mathbf1\)
  • \(\text{Var}(2X_1+X_2-5)=4\cdot9+5=\mathbf{41}\)

Example. Coating time: mean 35, var 11; rinsing: mean 8, var 5; independent. Total: mean \(=43\), var \(=16\), \(\sigma=\mathbf4\).

Proof 3: sample mean. \(X_1..X_n\) independent, same mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2\).

  • \(E(\bar X)=\frac1n\cdot n\mu=\mu\)
  • \(\text{Var}(\bar X)=\frac1{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2=\dfrac{\sigma^2}n\), so \(\sigma_{\bar X}=\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\) (this is the standard error used in Unit III)

Proof 4: sample variance is unbiased. \(E\left[\dfrac{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}{n-1}\right]=\sigma^2\). Outline:

  • Write \(X_i-\bar X=(X_i-\mu)-(\bar X-\mu)\) and expand.
  • \(\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=\sum(X_i-\mu)^2-n(\bar X-\mu)^2\)
  • Take expectations: \(E\sum(X_i-\mu)^2=n\sigma^2\) and \(E[n(\bar X-\mu)^2]=n\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}n=\sigma^2\)
  • So \(E\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=n\sigma^2-\sigma^2=(n-1)\sigma^2\), and dividing by \(n-1\) gives \(\sigma^2\) ✓. That is why the divisor is \(n-1\), not \(n\).
Explanation

These rules let you find a mean and variance without going back to a table or integral each time. The sum rule for means always works. The sum rule for variances needs independence, because if two variables move together their spread can add up or cancel.

The one that matters most for the later units is \(\text{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\): averaging \(n\) readings shrinks the spread by \(\sqrt n\). Every confidence interval and \(z\)-test in Unit III uses it. The \(n-1\) in the sample variance exists for a reason you can see in Proof 4: measuring deviations from \(\bar X\) instead of the true \(\mu\) makes them a little too small, and \(n-1\) corrects exactly that.
