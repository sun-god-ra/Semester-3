Joint Distributions: Continuous

Probability and Statistics · Unit II: Probability Densities and Joint Distributions

Joint Distributions: Continuous
Subject
Probability and Statistics (24MA203)
Unit
Unit II: Probability Densities and Joint Distributions
Topic
5 of 7
Terms
Expected value, Variance

Practice: Part B questions for Unit II and Part A short questions.

Notebook notes
  • Joint pdf \(f(x,y)\ge0\), with \(\displaystyle\iint f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=1\)
  • \(P\big((X,Y)\in A\big)=\displaystyle\iint_A f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\)
  • Joint cdf \(F(x,y)=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x}\!\int_{-\infty}^{y}f(u,v)\,dv\,du\)
  • Marginal pdfs (integrate out the other):
    • \(f_1(x)=\int f(x,y)\,dy\)
    • \(f_2(y)=\int f(x,y)\,dx\)
  • Conditional pdf \(f(y\mid x)=\dfrac{f(x,y)}{f_1(x)}\)
  • Independent iff \(f(x,y)=f_1(x)f_2(y)\) (also, limits must be a rectangle)
  • Rules for sums (imp):
    • \(E(aX+bY)=aE(X)+bE(Y)\) (always)
    • \(\text{Var}(X\pm Y)=\text{Var}(X)+\text{Var}(Y)\) (only if independent)

Example: \(f(x_1,x_2)=6e^{-2x_1-3x_2}\), \(x_1,x_2>0\)

  • \(f_1(x_1)=2e^{-2x_1}\), \(f_2(x_2)=3e^{-3x_2}\)
  • product \(=6e^{-2x_1-3x_2}=f\) ⇒ independent ✓
  • \(P(X_1<2,X_2>2)=(1-e^{-4})\,e^{-6}=0.002433\)
  • \(F(x_1,x_2)=(1-e^{-2x_1})(1-e^{-3x_2})\)

Sample mean (proof to remember): \(X_i\) iid with mean \(\mu\), var \(\sigma^2\)

  • \(E(\bar X)=\frac1n\cdot n\mu=\mu\)
  • \(\text{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\)
Explanation

The continuous version replaces the table with a surface \(f(x,y)\) and probabilities with volumes, which are double integrals. The marginal density of \(X\) is found by integrating \(y\) over its full range, and in the example this gives the exponential densities \(2e^{-2x_1}\) and \(3e^{-3x_2}\). Because the joint density factorises into these two marginals, the variables are independent, and this also lets you compute probabilities as a product of two single integrals.

Two results about sums are used again and again. The expected value of a sum is always the sum of expected values. The variance of a sum equals the sum of variances only for independent variables. Applying both to \(\bar X=\frac1n\sum X_i\) gives the facts that the sample mean is centred on \(\mu\) and that its variance shrinks like \(\sigma^2/n\). This is the starting point for statistical inference in the next unit.
