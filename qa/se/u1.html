Part B · Unit I

Process Models and Agile Development

Software Engineering · 9 questions · Notes for this unit

Q1Nov 20258MCO1 K2

Explain agility and the cost of change.

Answer

Agility is the ability of the team and process to respond quickly to change. An agile team accepts that requirements, technology and the team itself will change, and organises the work so that change is cheap. It means: effective communication with the customer, small self-organising teams, frequent delivery of working software in short increments, and a simple process that is adapted continuously.

Cost of change. In conventional processes the cost of making a change rises exponentially as the project moves on: a change during requirements costs little, a change during design costs more, and a change after testing or after release costs the most, because design, code, tests and documents all have to be redone.

(The numbers are only illustrative; the shape of the curve is the point.)

Agile claim. With good practices (iterative delivery, continuous integration, refactoring, test-driven development, simple design) the curve is flattened: the cost of a late change stays low, so there is no need to freeze everything up-front. (Kent Beck's argument for XP.) Pessimistic note: the flat curve is an aim, it only holds if automated tests and a clean design are really maintained.

Q2Nov 20258MCO1 K2

Explain prescriptive process models with a neat diagram.

Answer

Prescriptive models prescribe a set of process elements (framework activities, actions, tasks, work products, quality assurance, change control) and a workflow, to bring order to software work.

1. Waterfall (classic life cycle). Linear and sequential: Communication → Planning → Modeling → Construction → Deployment.

Good when requirements are stable and well understood. Weak: real projects rarely follow it, customer sees the product only at the end, blocking states.

2. Incremental model. Applies waterfall in staggered increments; each increment delivers an operational part. The first increment is the core product.

3. Evolutionary models.

  • Prototyping: build a quick prototype from the initial requirements, let the customer evaluate it, refine it; throw it away or evolve it.
  • Spiral: risk-driven; each loop around the spiral (communication, planning, modelling, construction, deployment) produces a more complete version, with risk analysis in every loop.

4. Concurrent model. Activities exist concurrently, each in a state (none, under development, awaiting changes, under review, done); events trigger state transitions.

5. Specialised models. Component-based development, formal methods, aspect-oriented development.

Choice depends on requirements stability, risk, size, and customer availability.

Q3Nov 20258MCO1 K2

(OR) Explain the principles of Agile development.

Answer

The Agile Manifesto values: individuals and interactions over processes and tools; working software over comprehensive documentation; customer collaboration over contract negotiation; responding to change over following a plan.

The 12 principles (summary):

  1. Satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software.
  2. Welcome changing requirements, even late in development.
  3. Deliver working software frequently (weeks rather than months).
  4. Business people and developers work together daily.
  5. Build projects around motivated individuals; trust them.
  6. Face-to-face conversation is the best way to communicate.
  7. Working software is the primary measure of progress.
  8. Sustainable development: a constant pace indefinitely.
  9. Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design.
  10. Simplicity: the art of maximising work not done.
  11. Self-organising teams produce the best architectures and designs.
  12. At regular intervals the team reflects and adjusts its behaviour.
Q4Nov 20258MCO1 K2

(OR) Explain the personal and team process models.

Answer

Personal Software Process (PSP) (Watts Humphrey) helps an individual engineer measure and improve his own work. Framework activities: Planning (size and resource estimates, defect estimates, schedule), High-level design, High-level design review, Development (detailed design, review, code, code review, compile, test) and Postmortem (compare actual with plan, update the process). Key idea: the engineer records time, size and defects and uses the data to estimate better and to remove defects early.

Team Software Process (TSP) builds self-directed teams of 3 to 20 engineers who plan and track their own work, set goals and own the process. Framework: Launch, Strategy, Plan, Requirements, Design, Implementation, Test, Postmortem; the team holds a launch meeting and a weekly progress meeting, has defined roles (team leader, development manager, planning manager, quality/process manager, support manager) and uses data collected through PSP.

Q5Expected8MCO1 K2

Explain Extreme Programming (XP) practices.

Answer

XP is the best-known agile method (Kent Beck). Values: communication, simplicity, feedback, courage, respect.

XP process activities: Planning (user stories written by the customer, each given a cost; the team picks stories for the next release), Design (KISS principle, CRC cards, spike solutions, refactoring), Coding (pair programming, unit tests written before the code), Testing (unit tests, daily integration, acceptance tests from user stories).

Key practices: small releases, simple design, test-driven development, pair programming, refactoring, collective code ownership, continuous integration, sustainable pace (40-hour week), on-site customer, coding standards, system metaphor.

Q6Apr 20268MCO1 K2

Explain the software development life cycle (SDLC).

Answer

SDLC is the structured sequence of phases used to build software from idea to retirement.

  1. Requirement analysis: find what the user needs; output is the SRS.
  2. Planning: feasibility, cost and effort estimate, schedule, risk and resources.
  3. Design: architecture, data, interface and component design; output is the design document.
  4. Implementation: coding according to the design and standards.
  5. Testing: unit, integration, system and acceptance testing; defects found are fixed.
  6. Deployment: release to the production environment, training.
  7. Maintenance: corrective, adaptive, perfective and preventive changes; usually the longest and costliest phase.
Q7Apr 20268MCO1 K2

Explain the phases of the Unified Process.

Answer

The UP is use-case driven, architecture-centric, iterative and incremental. It has four phases; each has iterations, and the five core workflows (requirements, analysis, design, implementation, test) run in them with different weight.

  • Inception: scope the project, identify main use cases (about 10 percent of them), a rough architecture, cost, schedule and risks; business case. Milestone: life-cycle objectives.
  • Elaboration: refine and expand the use cases, build the architectural baseline (about 80 percent of the use cases are detailed), mitigate the main risks. Milestone: life-cycle architecture.
  • Construction: develop the software components and features; the first operational beta. Milestone: initial operational capability.
  • Transition: beta testing, user training, defect correction, delivery to the end users. Milestone: product release.
  • Production: ongoing use, monitoring and support.
Q8Apr 20268MCO1 K2

(OR) Explain the advantages and limitations of Agile.

Answer

Advantages

  • Early and frequent delivery of working software; customer sees progress.
  • Changes are welcomed and cheap to absorb; reduces project risk.
  • Close collaboration improves understanding and satisfaction.
  • Motivated, self-organising teams; minimal paperwork; quick feedback and defect detection.

Limitations

  • Hard to estimate cost, effort and the final scope up front; fixed-price contracts are difficult.
  • Needs an available customer and experienced, disciplined team members.
  • Light documentation harms maintenance, new team members and regulated domains (safety critical, legal).
  • Scaling to large or distributed teams is difficult; architecture can degrade without discipline (technical debt).
  • Constant change can cause scope creep.
